Reinforcement learning came roaring back in 2025. One big reason is the rise of "reasoning" post-training: models like OpenAI's o1 series and DeepSeek's R1 are widely seen as benefiting from RL-based training, with noticeable boosts on math, coding, and many other tasks.

But here's the catch: what RL is actually doing in LLM post-training, especially in RLVR, still isn't settled. A lot of 2025 research on LLM reasoning revolves around a deceptively simple question: is RL mostly polishing the base model's existing knowledge (by reweighting the probabilities), or can it truly push the model beyond its original reasoning boundary and unlock new behaviors? In this post, we'll delve into these papers with that "sharpening or discovery" question in mind and take a more careful look on what we can conclude.

# Sharpening or Discovery? The Role of RL in LLM Reasoning

### A Quick Review of RLVR

We begin with a brief recap of **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**. Conceptually, RLVR is closely related to RLHF: both apply reinforcement learning during LLM post-training. The key difference is that, in RLVR, the reward signal can be *automatically verified*, without relying on human preferences or learned reward models.

For many tasks, such verifiable rewards arise naturally. In *mathematics*, the model's predicted answer $\hat{y}$ can be directly compared against the ground-truth solution $y$: if the answer is correct, the reward is set to 1; otherwise, it is 0. In *coding tasks*, correctness can be determined via compilation or unit tests. Along with *chain-of-thought (CoT)*, this setup makes it straightforward to evaluate whether a model's reasoning ultimately leads to a correct outcome.

Formally, we view a language model as a *policy* $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$, where $x$ denotes an input prompt (e.g., a math problem) and $y$ is the generated response. RLVR assumes access to a *verifier* $r(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$, which checks whether the output is correct. Crucially, this reward is independent of human preferences or learned reward models; it depends only on whether the final answer matches a deterministic criterion. In this sense, RLVR uses an *outcome reward*, rather than a process-level reward that evaluates intermediate reasoning steps.

In practice, RLVR is typically optimized with *policy-gradient methods* such as PPO, GRPO, REINFORCE++, and DAPO. As one concrete example, the DeepSeek models adopt **Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. GRPO optimizes the objective

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}[\hat{A}(x, y)]-\beta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}}[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x))].
$$

A few remarks help clarify this objective:

- **Advantage estimation.** The term $\hat{A}(x, y)$ is an estimate of the *advantage* of a sampled response, which is equivalent to reward maximization but with reduced variance. GRPO does not introduce a separate critic to estimate values. Instead, for a given prompt $x$, it samples a *group* of $G$ responses $y_1,\ldots,y_G$ (including $y$), obtains rewards $\{r_1, \ldots, r_G\}$, and computes a relative advantage

$$
\hat{A}(x, y)=\frac{r(x, y)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r_1, \ldots, r_G\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r_1, \ldots, r_G\})}.
$$

- **Behavior regularization via KL.** As also in PPO, the *KL term constrains* updates to stay close to a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, typically the pre-trained base model. This highlights a fundamental distinction between RL in LLMs and traditional RL. Traditional RL often starts *from scratch* and aims to balance exploration and exploitation. In contrast, RL for LLMs begins from a *strong pre-trained prior*. While this prior is widely believed to be a major source of performance gains, it also imposes a potentially restrictive constraint, one that will become central to our later discussion.

- **On-policy learning with verifiable rewards.** These algorithms learn exclusively from *on-policy samples*, i.e., responses generated by the current model. In the context of verifiable rewards, the training objective effectively increases the log-likelihood of responses with correct answers while decreasing the likelihood of incorrect ones.

In this post, we are going to delve into a debate on the role of RLVR in LLM post-training, which is highly active and popular in X. Since RLVR is trained on top of a *base model* that may already be quite strong and representative, a natural question is: compared with the base model, what exactly changes after RLVR post-training? What role does RLVR actually play? There are two different views:

1. **RLVR as a sharpener**: RLVR mainly *reweights* the base model's output distribution, making already-available correct reasoning trajectories more likely to be sampled, without expanding the model's underlying reasoning coverage.
2. **RLVR as a discoverer**: RLVR can *push beyond* the base model's edge-of-competence in certain domains, reinforcing rare but useful trajectories and enabling new generalization behaviors that the base model fails to reliably produce.

So, which view is closer to the truth? Below, we will walk through both perspectives by examing several representative papers, unpacking the logic behind them, and discussing what is actually at stake.

### RLVR as a "Sharpener"

In early 2025, several works argued that RLVR plays only a "sharpening" role. That is, RLVR mainly reweights the probability distribution of the base model's generations, pushing it toward responses that are already more correct and reliable, rather than creating fundamentally new reasoning abilities. This conclusion attracted wide attention after the NeurIPS 2025 Best Paper Runner-Up, the [limit-of-RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) paper, titled *Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?*.

The key metric used is **pass@k**, where the model is asked to sample $k$ responses; if at least one response is correct, pass@k is set to 1, otherwise 0. When $k$ is small, pass@k closely reflects deployment-time performance. When $k$ is large (e.g., $k = 128$), it can be viewed as a proxy for the model's reasoning coverage, namely the range of problems the model is capable of solving given sufficient sampling.

As shown in the paper (see the figure below), experiments are conducted across models of different scales (e.g., Qwen and LLaMA), different benchmarks about math and coding, and different RL algorithms (e.g., PPO, GRPO, REINFORCE++). The results reveal a consistent pattern:

> **RLVR significantly improves pass@k when $k$ is small. However, as $k$ increases, the pass@k of RL-trained models is eventually surpassed by that of the base models.**

This suggests that RLVR does not expand the reasoning boundary of the base model; instead, RL-trained models may exhibit narrower reasoning coverage than their base counterparts.

Notably, such observation is not first observed in this paper. As early as February 2024, the [DeepSeekMath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300) paper had already discussed why RL works in mathematical reasoning. The authors pointed out that RL improves *maj@k* (the majority vote among $k$ responses, similar to best-of-$N$), but not *pass@k*. This implies that the gains from RL come from boosting correct responses that already appear within the top-$k$ samples, rather than from enhancing the model's fundamental problem-solving capabilities.

These results demonstrate that RLVR appears to improve **sampling efficiency** rather than reasoning capability. In other words, everything RL produces is already something the base model can generate; RL simply makes it more likely that we obtain a correct answer with fewer samples. This is surprising because RL is often viewed as fundamentally different from purely data-driven learning. *In classical RL, the agent is expected to self-evolve*, discovering new paradigms from experience and achieving generalization beyond the training data. Yet here, instead of expanding the base model's coverage, RLVR seems to narrow it.

#### Is pass@k a good metric?

Some have questioned whether pass@k is actually a good metric. What it really measures is the coverage of correct outputs under a specific decoding policy, given $k$ tries. In that sense, it does reflect a form of reasoning boundary. However, there is an important caveat. It does not rule out situations where the model *knows* but cannot *say*. For example, a model may be able to judge whether an answer is correct, acting as a reasonably good critic, while still being unable to synthesize the solution from scratch. In such cases, a more advanced decoding strategy might be sufficient to resolve the issue.

For this reason, the [limit-of-RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) paper also discusses another metric, **perplexity**. Perplexity measures the log-likelihood of a given response under the model, and can be interpreted as how "surprised" the model is when seeing that answer. Lower perplexity indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the response. As shown in the figure below, compared to answers generated by the base model itself (first column), the answers produced by RL (second column) are less "surprising" to the base model. This suggests that the base model already contains the relevant primitives and assigns non-trivial probability to solving these problems. In this view, *RL merely sharpens that probability*.

That said, pass@k is a combinatorial metric. Suppose we sample $n \ge k$ i.i.d. responses, among which $c$ are correct. Then pass@k can be computed as

$$
\mathrm{pass@k} = 1 - \frac{\tbinom{n - c}{k}}{\tbinom{n}{k}}.
$$

Given that most RLVR methods are effectively optimizing pass@1, a natural question follows. Can we directly optimize pass@k instead? The [Pass@K Policy Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15201) (PKPO) paper explores this idea. By trading off different choices of $k$, they find that in regions where the base model has poor coverage, this approach can sometimes enable more effective exploration.

![perplexity](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/perlexity.png#width-50)

#### Why RLVR Has Limitations?

A brief summary of the "sharpening" perspective is that:

> **Current RLVR methods do not behave like traditional RL in the sense of exploring and discovering genuinely new capabilities. Instead, the capabilities of an RLVR-trained model are bounded by those of its base model.** That said, RLVR remains highly useful in practice, as it significantly improves sample efficiency. However, fully unlocking RL's potential to discover new knowledge or new reasoning strategies remains an open challenge.

Why does RLVR have such limitations? If we look back at traditional RL, for example RL-trained agents like AlphaGo, they were able to discover genuinely new strategies. A well-known example is *AlphaGo's 37th move*, which was recognized by human experts as a "new paradigm." The key differences between RLVR for LLMs and traditional RL (taking RL for Go as an example) mainly lie in two aspects.

1. The *action space* is different. If we consider the space of entire decision trajectories, RL for Go operates at a scale of roughly $\mathcal{O}(10^{768})$, whereas RL for LLMs operates at an even larger scale, around $\mathcal{O}(10^{10000})$.

2. The *prior* is different. Traditional RL is typically trained from scratch, while RLVR for LLMs starts from a very strong pre-trained prior, namely the base model.

Let us first focus on the second point. As discussed earlier, a strong base model is beneficial because it guides exploration and makes reward learning possible. However, to some extent, it also "limits" further exploration by RL. When we examine PPO-style algorithms more closely, we find that they all rely on the idea of a *trust region*, meaning that each update is constrained to be small. At the same time, the KL constraint in the optimization objective prevents the updated model from drifting too far away from the base model. As a result, if certain new paradigms lie far outside the support of the base model, such a prior, together with the training algorithm, naturally limits exploration toward those distant regions.

Now let us return to the first point. In such a vast action space, although both settings involve numbers far beyond astronomical scales, the action space in LLMs is so large that it becomes almost impossible for the algorithm to explore genuinely new regions. More concretely, RLVR algorithms rely on *naive token-level sampling* for exploration. This is a form of **passive exploration**, which stands in sharp contrast to the **active exploration** used in traditional RL, such as optimism-driven exploration in MCTS with PUCT. This observation suggests that, if we aim to expand the reasoning boundary, we may still need *strategic exploration* that actively and deliberately explores regions far away from the base model. A related concept is **curriculum learning**, where trajectories can be evolved from past exploration. One example is Google DeepMind's [AlphaEvolve](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/), which can be viewed as a self-evolving agent built on this idea.

### RLVR as a "Discoverer"

But is the current RLVR paradigm truly incapable of discovering new paradigms? In 2025, a series of follow-up works began to revisit this question. As discussed earlier, the answer appears to depend heavily on the *algorithmic mechanisms*, such as how exploration is performed and how parameters are updated. However, an important factor was largely overlooked in earlier analyses, including [limit-of-RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837): the role of the **task domain**.

Recall that [limit-of-RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) primarily evaluates RLVR on *math* and *coding* tasks. These domains appear extremely frequently during pre-training, and their rewards are relatively easy to design. It is also worth noting that DeepSeek R1 uses math and coding data extensively during SFT. In this sense, these domains are already very familiar to the base model.

As a result, for tasks where the base model is already highly competent, such as math and coding, we can reasonably argue that *the base model already has the ability to solve them*. RL then mainly acts as a "sharpener," increasing the probability of producing a correct answer. When we delve into the *benchmarks*, tasks such as AIME24 or MATH500 may not appear verbatim in pre-training, but the base model has already absorbed nearly all the relevant knowledge, methods, and techniques required to solve them (which we called **primitives**). Under metrics such as pass@128, it therefore becomes difficult to observe any meaningful improvement from RL.

What happens, then, when the base model is *not* familiar with the task or domain? This question is studied in NVIDIA's [ProRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864) paper. They introduce algorithmic modifications, such as maintaining a high rollout temperature to mitigate entropy collapse, enabling what they call *prolonged reinforcement learning* (ProRL), with training lasting $>2000$ steps. They evaluate this approach across multiple domains, with results shown below.

![ProRL](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/prorl.png#width-70)

Their findings reveal a clear contrast. In *math* and *coding* domains, the base model already achieves high pass@128, and RL yields little to no improvement in reasoning boundary. In some cases, particularly in math, the RL-trained model is even surpassed by the base model, as indicated by the "Diminished Area" in the figure. In contrast, ProRL shows substantial gains on the [Reasoning Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24760) benchmark (gray points in the figure), which consists of logic puzzles, reasoning games, and other structured reasoning tasks. In these domains, the base model's pass@128 is typically low, while RL significantly expands the reasoning boundary.

Below are examples of tasks from the [Reasoning Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24760) benchmark:

- *maze*: given a grid maze with a start, goal, walls, and paths, find a feasible path, output the shortest path length, or produce the action sequence.
- *dice*: combinatorial probability and counting problems involving dice.
- *palindrome*: e.g., checking whether a string or number is a palindrome, finding minimal edits to make it a palindrome, or counting palindromic substrings.
- *boxnet*: connectivity and inference problems defined over box, grid, or graph-like structures.

Another key observation from the [ProRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864) paper concerns **out-of-distribution (OOD) data**. For certain tasks such as *boxnet*, which are essentially absent from pre-training, the base model achieves near-zero performance across all pass@k values. In contrast, the ProRL-trained model shows dramatic improvements, with pass@k approaching 1 in some cases, as shown below.

![ProRL boxnet](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/prorl-boxnet.png#width-70)

This suggests that RLVR models do exhibit a degree of *generalization* (which we will extend later). As ProRL training proceeds, the generated trajectories become increasingly novel, with less overlap. Tasks where RL provides little gain tend to be those with lower *creativity*, meaning they closely resemble patterns already seen during pre-training.

The [GURU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14965) paper further systematizes this observation by showing that the effects of RL are strongly *domain-dependent*. They construct a truly multi-domain RL-reasoning dataset, named GURU, spanning six reasoning domains: *math*, *code*, *science* (basic natural science questions), *logic* (simple logical reasoning games), *simulation* (execution-style reasoning without actually running programs), and *tabular* (numerical reasoning over structured tables). They report two key findings:

> For *math, code, and science*, performance improves even when RL training data comes from other domains. This suggests that the relevant capabilities already exist in the base model, and RL mainly serves to *elicit* or reweight them.

> For *logic, simulation, and tabular reasoning*, cross-domain RL is largely ineffective. Performance improves only with **in-domain RL**, which is more consistent with RL discovering reasoning skills that the model has barely encountered during pre-training.

![GURU](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/guru.png)

Taken together, these results suggest that RL for LLM reasoning is neither purely exploitation nor purely exploration. Instead, RL operates within a space shaped by *pre-training coverage*, playing very different roles across domains. In this sense, these findings partially challenge the conclusion of the [limit-of-RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) paper. There do exist tasks that the base model fails to solve but RL-trained models can handle, although this phenomenon is highly dependent on the pre-training domain.

A closer look at task types reveals that domains such as *logic*, which involve logical constraints and reasoning games, benefit particularly strongly from RL. This is interesting, because logic problems are often assumed to be similar to math problems, yet they differ in important ways. Logic tasks are closer to *constraint satisfaction with global consistency*. One must satisfy a collection of discrete constraints, such as adjacency, ordering, or uniqueness, to arrive at a globally consistent assignment. As a result, correct solutions are often sparse, brittle, and discrete: violating a single constraint leads to complete failure. When running RL via policy-gradient methods, once a valid trajectory is discovered, the gradient signal is strong and can push the model rapidly toward the feasible solution region.

In contrast, *math* problems, especially competition-style benchmarks like AIME, are often more *local* in nature. The base model may have already seen isomorphic problems or enough intermediate lemmas during pre-training, leaving RL with limited room beyond improving sample efficiency. How to formally model this difference, and how to explicitly characterize RL's role in domains such as logic, remains an open problem.

### On the Interplay of Pre-Training, Mid-Training, and RLVR

Putting everything together, the role of RLVR is often framed as a debate between two perspectives: **"sharpener"** versus **"discoverer."** In the second half of 2025, many works revisited this "sharpening-or-discovery" question, including [RL-learns-composing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25123), [art-of-scaling-rl](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786), and [BroRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01180), among others. Most of their conclusions are broadly consistent with the discussion above. A recent paper, [interplay](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.07783), titled *On the Interplay of Pre-Training, Mid-Training, and RL on Reasoning Language Models*, became quite popular on X. It conducts a fully controllable study of this question AT A SMALL SCALE (Note: NOT SCALING UP) and provides a set of rather convincing conclusions.

Their approach is to construct tasks using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). Each task is defined by an underlying DAG composed of atomic operations such as $+-\times\div$, with an explicit step-by-step reasoning trace. The difficulty of a problem is controlled by the number of operations (op), while a contextual template $\tau$ is used to control the problem's context. This setup allows the data to be divided into three categories:

- *ID data*: op = 2-10, fully covered by pre-training
- *OOD-edge (OOD-mid) data*: op = 11-14, just beyond pre-training coverage, where the base model still has non-zero pass@128
- *OOD-hard data*: op = 15-20, where the base model's performance is near zero (pass@k is almost zero)

They pre-train a 100M Qwen2.5-style model on 10B tokens. Post-training is then performed on different data regimes (shown as different colored curves): ID, mixed (op = 9-12), OOD-edge, and OOD-hard. Finally, all models are evaluated on ID, OOD-mid, and OOD-hard test sets, corresponding to the three plots below.

![interplay 1](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/interplay-1.png)

Several patterns stand out. In the ID regime, since the tasks lie squarely within the base model's pre-training coverage, increasing $k$ in pass@k actually allows the base model to outperform the RL-trained model. This aligns well with the findings of [limit-of-RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837). However, when we move to the OOD-edge regime (the middle plot), models that undergo RL post-training on OOD-edge data (or mixed data) achieve huge performance improvements over the base model. This leads to a clear takeaway:

> **RL produces true capacity gains (pass@128) beyond the base model only when two conditions hold:**

1. The task is *not* heavily covered during pre-training, leaving sufficient headroom for exploration.
2. The RL data is calibrated to the model's **edge-of-competence**, neither too easy (in-distribution) nor too hard (out-of-distribution).

One way to interpret this is as follows. On in-distribution data, the base model has already learned most of the relevant knowledge, so it is natural for RLVR to act primarily as a "sharpener." At the same time, RL data cannot be arbitrarily difficult. When RL post-training is performed directly on OOD-hard data, the resulting model still fails to improve on OOD-hard tasks (as shown in the rightmost plot). The intuition here echoes what we discussed earlier under **curriculum learning**: models need to learn in a gradual, staged manner.

An intuitive analogy is to think of ID data as elementary-school material, OOD-edge data as middle-school problems, and OOD-hard data as high-school problems. If a model learns middle-school problems during post-training, it may generalize and solve some high-school problems at test time. But if we train directly on high-school problems, the model may fail to learn anything at all. This suggests that RL data must be designed around the model's *edge-of-competence*, which is itself a challenging problem in practice. How to formalize this process and build a principled theoretical framework around it remains an important open question.

#### Generalization and Primitives

In fact, the so-called "sharpening-or-discovery" debate is fundamentally about whether RL can improve a model's **generalization** ability. Based on the [interplay](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.07783) paper, we would like models to exhibit two types of generalization:

- **Extrapolative generalization**: when the reasoning structure becomes more complex (longer CoT, deeper dependencies), can the model *extrapolate*?
- **Contextual generalization**: when the reasoning structure stays the same but the linguistic surface form or context changes, can the model *transfer*?

Under the setting of the [interplay](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.07783) paper, *extrapolative generalization* can be tested by varying the number of operations (op). Concretely, this asks whether a model trained only on data with smaller op can correctly solve reasoning tasks with larger op at test time. This leads back to the earlier conclusion: when RL data is positioned at the base model's *edge-of-competence*, RL can enhance the model's extrapolative generalization.

Similarly, contextual generalization is controlled by varying the contextual templates of the problems. The paper conducts corresponding experiments and finds the following pattern. Without minimal pre-training exposure to a new context, RL cannot induce transfer. However, even sparse exposure (e.g., $\ge 1\%$) provides a sufficient seed that RL can reinforce during post-training, yielding robust cross-context generalization. In other words, **RL incentivizes contextual generalization only when the base model already contains the necessary primitives**.

![interplay 2](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/interplay-2.png)

This also reflects a fact that RL does not create knowledge itself, which is very reasonable! When the base model already contains the relevant primitives, RL primarily strengthens and recomposes these existing primitives to achieve contextual generalization. This suggests that RL is not the source of *semantics* itself, but rather operates on top of existing semantic and reasoning primitives. The [RL-learns-composing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25123) paper explores this idea explicitly, showing that RL can learn *compositions* such as $f(g(x))$ from primitives $f(x)$ and $g(x)$.

At a deeper level, the core difficulty we discussed earlier remains the challenge of *sampling* in a vast action space. If the required primitive does not exist, certain reasoning paths may be sampled with almost zero probability under the base policy, so that the probability of a successful trajectory is $p = \Pr_{\pi_\theta}(R = 1) \approx 0$. In this case, for a new context, the model effectively never observes positive examples. The resulting gradients are dominated by noise, making it extremely difficult for policy-gradient methods to learn the corresponding reasoning paths.

Of course, what exactly means as a "primitive"? In the [interplay](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.07783) paper, primitives are explicit due to the synthetic environment and oracle verifier. In real LLMs, however, a "primitive" may be much more implicit: a local pattern, a syntactic template, a self-check or reflection format, or a language-level representation of a search strategy. As long as the probability of receiving a positive signal can be increased from $0$ to some small $\epsilon$, RL can start to work. This also explains the paper's finding that only about ~1% exposure is sufficient for transfer: the seed can be extremely weak.

#### The Role of Mid-Training

The [interplay](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.07783) paper also analyzes the role of so-called **mid-training**. Mid-training is a new term introduced in 2025 by papers, e.g., [OctoThinker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20512) and [midtraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14865). It refers to an intermediate phase between pre-training and post-training, designed to *bridge data distributions* and *strengthen reasoning priors* before downstream tasks. In practice, mid-training uses high-quality, domain-specific data in a *supervised* manner, similar to *Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)*. However, its role in the overall training pipeline is not exactly the same as SFT, and we will not go into the difference between mid-training and SFT here.

To make the comparison fair, they fix a *compute budget* and study how to allocate it between mid-training and RL, i.e., what balance is needed. For both mid-training and RL, they train on data with op = 11-14, which sits at the base model's edge-of-competence. The results are shown below. They find that:

- *Light-RL* (along with heavy mid-training): it achieves the *highest pass@1 on edge-of-competence tasks* (OOD-edge). This suggests that mid-training can effectively consolidate core skills and improve stability.
- *Heavy-RL* (along with light mid-training): it performs best on the *hardest tasks* (OOD-hard). More RL exploration appears crucial for tackling deeper reasoning patterns that the model has essentially never mastered before.

![interplay 3](/notes/role-of-rl-in-llm-reasoning/interplay-3.png)

The takeaway is that **introducing a mid-training phase that bridges pre- and post-training distributions can substantially strengthen generalization under a fixed compute budget**. When we prioritize *in-distribution performance*, we can allocate more budget to mid-training with only light RL. For *out-of-distribution generalization*, it is better to reserve a modest portion of compute for mid-training to establish essential priors, and dedicate the remaining budget to heavier RL exploration.

Most academic attention today focuses on RL-centered post-training. These results suggest that *mid-training is an underexplored but powerful lever in training design*. Models such as Qwen and LLaMA are also increasingly adopting mid-training as a dedicated phase in the pipeline, using it to align supervision more closely with the post-training distribution. This actually resembles an **offline-to-online** perspective. A core difficulty in RL is *distribution mismatch*, and mid-training offers a supervised way to bridge distributions, giving the model some exposure to the target distribution (e.g., the optimal policy's occupancy distribution) before RL begins. This can help mitigate distribution mismatch in practice, and it is also a direction that seems worth modeling and understanding more formally.

## Final Thoughts and Discussion

We have revisited a deceptively simple question that has sparked intense debate in 2025: Does RLVR truly expand the reasoning capacity of LLMs beyond the base model, or does it merely polish what is already there? As the evidence accumulates, a clear picture begins to emerge. This is not a yes-or-no question. The answer is **"or."** Whether RLVR behaves as a **sharpener** or a **discoverer** depends critically on multiple factors, including the *task domain*, the *pre-training coverage*, the *RL data*, and some *algorithmic reasons*.

A key takeaway is that *RL in LLMs remains fundamentally different from classical RL*. In traditional RL settings such as Go, exploration can lead to the discovery of genuinely new strategies and paradigms. In contrast, RLVR for LLMs does not appear to explore new semantics, new concepts, or new primitives in the same sense. Instead, exploration happens within a space already spanned by the base model's **reasoning primitives**. RL searches over this space to identify which *compositions*, *orders*, and longer trajectories of existing primitives lead to successful outcomes. In this view, RLVR is less about inventing new building blocks, and more about discovering which complex combinations of existing blocks are feasible and reliable.

Ultimately, this is a debate about **generalization**. RLVR can improve *extrapolative generalization* when it operates near the model's edge-of-competence, allowing the model to handle deeper or longer reasoning chains than those seen during pre-training. It can also improve *contextual generalization*, but only when the base model already contains the necessary primitives, even if only weakly. RL does not create these primitives from scratch; it amplifies and recomposes them. This prespective helps reconcile many seemingly contradictory results in the literature and highlights why RL's effects are so strongly domain-dependent.

Taken together, these observations suggest that RLVR should not be viewed as a monolithic mechanism with a single, fixed role. Rather, it is a flexible tool whose impact is jointly shaped by *pre-training coverage*, *mid-training design*, *the choice of RL data*, and *the underlying algorithmic structure*. Understanding how these components interact is essential if we want to move beyond empirical back-and-forth and toward more principled progress. From a theoretical perspective, this naturally leads to several viewpoints and questions worth exploring:

- **Understanding the role of the base model in RL.** How should we reason about RL when it is applied on top of a strong pre-trained prior, rather than learning from scratch? For example, [role-of-base-model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07453) and [coverage-principle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15020) discuss how the base model, through a coverage condition, can shape what is *computationally accessible* during RL.

- **Strategic exploration to expand the reasoning boundary.** What forms of exploration are necessary for RL to move beyond the base model's edge-of-competence, instead of merely refining what is already accessible? If we look back at preference optimization in RLHF, papers such as [XPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21046) introduce an optimistic bonus to explicitly encourage exploration.

- **Understanding the optimization mechanism of RL.** What is RL actually optimizing in LLM post-training, and how does this connect to generalization, stability, and reasoning behavior? For instance, Meta's recent [the-path-not-taken](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08567) proposes a three-gate theory suggesting that RLVR tends to produce smoother and more local updates.

Of course, there are many other promising directions. For example, the role of *mid-training* naturally offers an offline-to-online perspective, while *curriculum learning* provides a mechanism for gradually expanding the model's competence. How to model these components theoreticallyand give concrete algorithmic guidance remains an open and pressing challenge.

Finally, a crucial aspect of RLVR is that it relies on **outcome rewards rather than process-level rewards**. Many of the papers discussed earlier touch on this distinction in different ways. In practice, this choice is closely tied to issues such as sparse rewards and reward hacking, and it fundamentally shapes how learning signals propagate through long reasoning trajectories. How to properly understand and address this trade-off is an important question in its own right.
